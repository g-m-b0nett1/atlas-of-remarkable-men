Atlas of Remarkable Men

“[…] you have worked, suffered and laboured incessantly, and not once did you decide for yourself or know how to work so that, if only for a few months, the desire of your mind should become the desire of your heart.”

The old Tamil of the Sarmoung to Prince Lubovedsky, Ch. VII

In the last few months, during the frequent bookshops raids for which I am famous among my peers, my attention has often fallen victim to a book that, what with its pleasing cover, the curious attire of the man depicted on it and maybe the fortunate disposition on a shelf of the item itself, now rests somewhat ruffled in my library. I am talking about Georges Ivanovich Gurdjieff's Meetings with Remarkable Men. It's a picaresque Bildungsroman dense with names and locations lying on the belt from Transcaucasia to Turkestan with which I had little familiarity. Despite the inviting covers and excellent typeset that are typical of its publishing house, I was left wanting of a map and a glossary that would make my experience more complete and especially, would avoid me the rotten habit of picking up my smartphone at every page turn to check what I do not know. Supposing this is a shared feeling out there, I set out to gather the locations and journeys that Gurdjieff names in the book into an interactive map and ended up writing a whole compendium to the book, together with my own elucubrations, which I hope the reader will excuse.

This page is in two parts. The first, Tools for reading, is meant to complete (without, I hope, coming across as overly presumptuous) the experience of the book: an interactive map of the places and journeys, which can be narrowed to a single chapter; a web of the remarkable men and a list of everyone else the book names; a timeline of its dates; a note on how the two editions at my disposal spell their names; and a glossary. The second, Analysis and miscellanea, deals with Gurdjieff's persona, the way others perceived him and the role of Meetings in his work, then reads the book from several angles: how it handles place and time, what it makes of the occult fashions of its day, the uses of its tall tales, what it sets out to teach and how much of that it leaves unsaid. It ends with the book’s afterlives in music and in the work of later writers and musicians, and with what has become of its teaching in an age we can safely name, the reign of quantity.

A note on editions. I read Meetings with Remarkable Men in the Italian translation published by Adelphi (Incontri con uomini straordinari, 1977) and compared it throughout with the English edition (Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1963), which is the reference for this page: names, spellings, readings and page numbers follow the latter text for the sake of internationality, and the former is mentioned only where the two disagree. The Italian was translated from the French edition (Julliard, 1960), the first to be published; the English from Gurdjieff’s Russian manuscript. Unfortunately, I am not much versed in French nor in Russian.

SPOILERS. The page recounts the book’s episodes in full, endings included. Readers who would rather meet the remarkable men as Gurdjieff introduces them may prefer to read the book first and come back to the map with it in hand.

Incontri con uomini straordinari

Meetings with Remarkable Men is the second part of George I. Gurdjieff’s trilogy All and Everything. He began writing it in Russian in 1927, revised it for years, and left it to be published after his death. It presents itself as an answer to the question he was most often asked, and of which apparently he had had enough of answering to: which remarkable men had he met in his life?, The book tells, through a series of portraits, of his childhood in Armenia and some twenty years of travel through the Caucasus, the Near East, Egypt and Central Asia. His companions, the Seekers of Truth, are engineers, doctors, a prince and an archaeologist, all looking for an ancient knowledge and a deeper meaning to life they believe has survived in hidden monasteries and brotherhoods.

The book reads as an adventure story, but it was written to teach. It mixes precise, verifiable detail with episodes no one has been able to confirm, and places the lessons that matter in conversations, anecdotes and jokes, each chapter built to make the reader question the way they live.

    Notes on the map

    I pinpointed the places mentioned in the book, to the best of my skill, as I read the Italian text. I took note of every town, river, region and monastery it mentions, checking each against the English edition, and assigning modern coordinates; where Gurdjieff names a region rather than a town, the pin sits in the region. Lines join the stops in the order the book gives them; they are direct links, not reconstructed roads. Borders and capitals of about 1900 are drawn for orientation and are approximate, since they fix a single moment in a period when Russian Central Asia, the frontiers of Persia and the Ottoman provinces were all shifting.

    Gurdjieff hides several places behind initials (the towns “Z”, “N” and “P”, the village “F”, the “waterfall of K”), swears not to reveal the way to the Sarmoung monastery, and names rivers and ruins no one has identified, such as Yeni-Hissar and the river “Sharakshan”. These appear as dashed pins I placed by conjecture. A few statements do not fit the geography: a journey from Babylon “through Meshed to Ispahan”, Afridis in the heart of Kafiristan, a “Gobi” left at Keriya on the edge of the Taklamakan. They are mapped as written, and their cards say what does not fit.

    Page numbers are those of the English edition, read in the Internet Archive scan of the 1971 paperback. The photographs in the place cards are period views, most of them by Sergei Prokudin-Gorsky, taken some years after Gurdjieff’s journeys.

    TeachersCompanions and Seekers of TruthOthers
    Gurdjieff

    (Ch. I), narrator. George Ivanovich Gurdjieff, the book’s narrator and author, born in Alexandropol; the date is disputed, and the preface of the Adelphi edition, translated from the French, gives 1 January 1877. After some twenty years of travel he appeared in Russia in 1913, founded the Institute for the Harmonious Development of Man, and settled at the Prieuré near Paris in 1922. He died in Paris in 1949.

    Adash

    (Ch. II, p. 32), Gurdjieff’s father. The name, from the Turkic adaş, “namesake”, under which Gurdjieff’s father was known as a bard. A Greek from a family that had fled Byzantium, he was rich in herds until a cattle plague from Asia killed them, including thousands of head entrusted to him by poorer families, whose losses he repaid, ruining himself; he then became a carpenter in Alexandropol and Kars. He recited the Gilgamesh epic from memory, and Gurdjieff was later struck to find that it matched the Babylonian tablets deciphered in his lifetime. Gurdjieff last saw him in 1916, aged eighty-two. He was wounded defending his house when the Turks attacked Alexandropol and died soon after.

    Borsh

    (Ch. II, p. 34), Gurdjieff’s first tutor. Dean of the Russian military cathedral of Kars and the highest spiritual authority of a region only recently taken from the Ottomans, about seventy, a composer of church music. He took the chorister Gurdjieff under his wing, found him teachers in science and religion, and became his father’s closest friend; the two old men spent nights in the workshop debating legends and inventing the kastusilia. Gurdjieff calls him the creator of his individuality.

    Bogachevsky / Bogačevsky

    (Ch. IV, p. c. 58), Gurdjieff’s second teacher. A young deacon at the Kars cathedral, waiting for ordination, who taught Gurdjieff and heard his weekly confession. He taught that there are two moralities, an objective one given by nature and conscience and a subjective one of local custom, illustrating it with cases from the Kars garrison. After leaving his chaplaincy in Transcaspia, reportedly because of his wife’s infidelity, he became a monk, went on to Athos and Jerusalem, and joined the Essene brotherhood, rising under the name Father Evlissi to assistant to the abbot of its chief monastery.

    Pogossian, Sarkis / Sarkiss

    (Ch. V, p. 78), Armenian friend. A seminarian from Kars, son of a dyer and an embroiderer from Erzerum, met at Echmiadzin, who gave up the priesthood to share Gurdjieff’s search. After the voyage from Smyrna he stayed in a warship’s engine room, studied engineering in England, and ended as a shipowner commanding his own vessel in the East Indies. On the desert crossing he devised wooden pack-saddles that served as firewood. Gurdjieff calls him Mr X and holds him up as a man who succeeded in business without losing his soul.

    Yelov, Abram

    (Ch. VI, p. c. 109), bookseller. An Aisor from Bitlis or thereabouts who sold books in Tiflis, with a phenomenal knowledge of books and later of languages. He and Pogossian bickered affectionately for years, and on the desert crossing he proposed, half in jest, that the sheep should carry the travellers’ stilts and the travellers themselves. He became a bookseller across Russia, was ruined in the Revolution while in Siberia, and was brought to America by a nephew.

    Lubovedsky, Prince Yuri

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 118), Russian prince. A rich Russian officer of the Guards who, after his young wife died in childbirth, turned to spiritualism, the fashionable craze of the day, in the hope of reaching her; disappointed, he gave his life and fortune to the search for truth in Africa, India, Afghanistan and Persia. Much older than Gurdjieff, he was his closest friend for thirty-five years. Gurdjieff found him in the Sarmoung monastery, from which he left for the Olman monastery to spend his last three years.

    Ekim Bey

    (Ch. VIII, p. c. 177), Turkish friend. Son of a pasha, a student at a military school in Germany, then Turkey’s ally in modernising its army, when Gurdjieff met him in Constantinople. He became a doctor with a passion for hypnotism and the power of thought, so sought after that answering his letters would have needed fifty secretaries. He travelled with the Seekers through Persia and lived out his life in Egypt with the title of a great pasha.

    Karpenko, Piotr

    (Ch. VII, p. 167), mining engineer. Gurdjieff’s boyhood rival in Kars, wounded in the duel the two boys fought, after a scuffle the day before, by lying in hollows on the artillery range while the gunners fired. He became a mining engineer and geologist, joined the Seekers after an excavation at Ani, fell ill on the Persian expedition, proposed the plan for feeding the flock on the desert crossing, a mixture of sand, minced mutton and salt, and was wounded on the raft below the Pamirs, the cause of his early death.

    Skridlov

    (Ch. VI), archaeologist. A Russian professor of archaeology, first Gurdjieff’s client at the pyramids, then his companion at Thebes, up the Nile, in Abyssinia, at Babylon and, disguised as a dervish, in Kafiristan. After meeting Father Giovanni he said he had lost his egoism and wept easily from tenderness. Their last conversation, on Mount Beshtau during the First World War, ends the book’s portraits.

    Soloviev / Solovëv

    (Ch. VII, p. 134), Seeker of Truth. The son of a Samara merchant family who drifted into drink after following a circus rider, worked as a clerk and railway soldier in Transcaspia, and was caught up in a counterfeiting scheme before Gurdjieff met him in New Bukhara. He became an authority on Eastern and Tibetan medicine and on the effects of opium and hashish, went with Gurdjieff to the Sarmoung, and was killed by a wild camel in the desert in 1898.

    Vitvitskaia

    (Ch. VII, p. 124), Seeker of Truth. A Polish woman, daughter of an estate manager, seduced, robbed and then lured by her own brother into the traffic in women that the period called the white slave trade, which shipped young women from Eastern Europe to the brothels of the Ottoman Empire and South America. The Prince rescued her on the way to Alexandria. She joined the Seekers, travelled with them in Asia, and devoted herself to the effect of music on people. She caught a chill on a sleigh journey on the Volga, died, and was buried in Samara.

    Sari-Ogli / Sari Oglé

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 170), doctor. A Persian from eastern Persia educated in France. Climbing a ladder during a sandstorm, he found the air clear of sand at about seven metres, and proposed that the Seekers cross the desert on stilts above the storms. He travelled with Gurdjieff in Persia and turned back with him toward Khorasan.

    Dashtamirov / Dachtamirov

    (Ch. VII, p. 175), astronomer. A Seeker of Truth and an experienced astronomer, who guided the desert crossing at night by the stars.

    Nijeradze / Nijeradzé, Prince

    (Ch. VIII, p. c. 191), Seeker of Truth. A prince, by his surname Georgian, whose fever, with Karpenko’s, turned the Persian expedition toward Baghdad.

    Vasiliaki / Vassiliaki

    (Ch. X, p. c. 228), Greek friend. A Greek tailor who had long lived in Merv and wore Tekke dress, one of the many Greeks scattered through Russian Asia. He pulled Gurdjieff off the train to a christening feast where the plan for Kafiristan was born.

    Father Giovanni

    (Ch. X, p. c. 238), monk of the Universal Brotherhood. A former Catholic missionary from Italy who, travelling in Afghanistan, was taken prisoner by the Afridis and passed from hand to hand as a slave until he found the Brotherhood and stayed. His talks on faith, which he said cannot be given to anyone but only arise from understanding, transformed Skridlov.

    Bogga-Eddin / Boga Eddin

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 135), dervish. Gurdjieff’s old friend in Bukhara, who introduced him to the old man of the Sarmoung and arranged the journey to its monastery. His name is a form of Baha ud-Din, the name of Bukhara’s patron saint, Baha ud-Din Naqshband, founder of the Naqshbandi Sufi order, which may be deliberate.

    The Persian dervish

    (Ch. VIII, p. c. 191), teacher. An old dervish in rags in a village beyond Tabriz. He mocked Gurdjieff’s Hatha Yoga habit of chewing each mouthful endlessly, quoting a Persian saying against those who teach what they do not know, and his gymnastics, which in his view developed only the outer muscles, and explained to Ekim Bey how to care for the body; Gurdjieff says the meeting overturned his outlook.

    The old man of the Sarmoung

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 156), envoy. An old man in the dress of a Tamil, from southern India or Ceylon, whom the Prince met in Kabul. He called the Prince by his childhood nickname, Gogo, known only to his mother and nurse, and reproached him for the way he had spent his life; through him the Prince reached the Sarmoung. Bogga-Eddin also met a member of the brotherhood in Bukhara.

    Others named in the book

    The Afghan hermit

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 221), hermit. An old man from Khorasan, once chief of artillery to the Emir of Afghanistan, wounded in a rising of Afghans and Baluchis. He then sought out Persian dervishes, was for a time a Baptist, entered a monastery near Kabul, and finally lived alone in the mountains, where he advised the lost Seekers how to get out of the gorges.

    Aga Khan

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 156), host in Kabul. The hereditary title of the imam of the Nizari Ismaili Muslims. The Prince calls him an old friend and meets the old man of the Sarmoung at his house in Kabul. The Aga Khans of the time lived in Bombay, so the setting is unexpected; the book does not say which Aga Khan is meant.

    Ahl and Sez / Akhel and Seze

    (Ch. X, p. 241), brothers of the Universal Brotherhood. Two very old monks, Sez said to be three hundred years old, who travel between the order’s four monasteries preaching on aspects of the divine. Father Giovanni contrasts them to illustrate a law of understanding: Sez speaks like a bird of paradise and enchants at once, but the effect fades; Ahl speaks poorly and indistinctly, but what he says grows in the listener with time.

    Andronik / Andronikov

    (Ch. V, p. c. 92), Armenian hero. A young fighter for Armenian freedom whose name was on everyone’s lips when Gurdjieff and Pogossian set out, and who later became a national hero. He is almost certainly Andranik Ozanian (1865–1927), a guerrilla leader against the Ottomans in the 1890s and later a general, which dates the journey to that decade of Armenian revolt and Ottoman reprisal.

    Arem and Telvant / Telvent

    (Ch. V, p. 89), monks in the Ani letter. Father Arem is the addressee of the old letters found at Ani; Father Telvent is the monk who, the letter says, learned the truth about the Sarmoung. Neither is otherwise known.

    Baron X / Baron F

    (Ch. IX, p. 209), Seeker of Truth. An ardent occultist, one of the Seekers, killed with the guide Karakir Khainu in an avalanche while they tried to cross from the Pamirs to India. The two editions even disagree on his initial.

    Djek

    (Ch. VII), dog. Prince Lubovedsky’s fox terrier, who would take no sugar from strangers without his master’s leave; when a young woman on the boat tried to feed him, the Prince began the acquaintance that led to Vitvitskaia’s rescue.

    The elderly Persian

    (Ch. I, p. c. 6), speaker. An intelligent old Persian whom Gurdjieff heard in his youth, whose long speech on the emptiness of contemporary literature, journalism and European civilisation fills most of the Introduction. He is Gurdjieff’s mouthpiece for his own contempt for literary style without substance.

    Father Yevlampios / Eulampios

    (Ch. V, p. 79), monk. A celebrated monk of the monastery of Sanaine, to whom the young Gurdjieff was an acolyte for three months during his search for answers among the holy places of Transcaucasia.

    Karakir Khainu

    (Ch. IX, p. 209), guide. The guide who knew the mountains between the Pamirs and the Himalayas, killed in the avalanche with Baron X. The loss of a guide in country with no maps left the Seekers lost in the gorges.

    Kerensky, Petia

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 202), boyhood friend. A friend from Alexandropol, son of a postal inspector who was later killed in the Russo-Japanese war of 1904–05.

    Korkhanidi / Khorkanidi

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 202), boyhood friend. A boy from the Greek quarter of Kars, nicknamed Fekhi, who later wrote many school textbooks; Kars had a large Greek community, to which Gurdjieff’s family belonged.

    Makarov

    (Ch. IV, p. 74), officer in Kars. A lieutenant who shot himself over a card debt to a notorious cheat, Captain Mashvelov (Mačvelov in the Adelphi edition). Under the officers’ code of honour a gambling debt had to be paid whatever its origin, and Bogachevsky uses the case, set against another where an officer was acquitted of blinding a cobbler, to show how conventional morality works.

    Mardiross

    (Ch. IV, p. c. 62), fortune-teller. Eung-Ashokh Mardiross, a half-witted wandering bard and fortune-teller whom Gurdjieff’s aunt consulted about his future. Some of his predictions came true, one of several unexplained events that pushed the young Gurdjieff toward his search.

    Mullah Nassr Eddin

    (Ch. I, p. c. 2), folk sage. The legendary wise fool of Turkish, Persian and Arab folklore, Nasreddin Hodja, traditionally said to have lived in Anatolia in the 13th century, the hero of countless jokes in which foolishness hides wisdom. Gurdjieff quotes his sayings throughout, often of his own invention, and uses him as a model for teaching through humour.

    Pasha N

    (Ch. VIII, p. c. 181), Ekim Bey’s father. A former district governor who lost an amber rosary set with diamonds off the Galata bridge. When the coin-diver Gurdjieff returned it and then collapsed, he took him into his house and had him nursed, which is where Gurdjieff met his son.

    Philos

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 135), dog. Gurdjieff’s huge Kurdish sheepdog, whose name is Greek for “friend”, his companion for nine years and famous wherever they went. He fetched boiling water from teahouses, stole Jerusalem artichokes for his master and brought him dead sparrows.

    Ponomarenko / Ponomerenko and Krestovsky

    (Ch. III, p. 53), early teachers. Two seminarians waiting to become army chaplains, whom Dean Borsh engaged to teach Gurdjieff geography, history, catechism and Russian, alongside the doctor Sokolov, who taught him anatomy and physiology.

    Sando

    (Ch. II, p. c. 43), hypnotic subject. A simple-minded Armenian woman on whom Gurdjieff, in Alexandropol, demonstrated the transfer of sensation at a distance: a pin stuck into a wax figure smeared with her oil drew blood from the same place on her body. His father later used the memory to explain his own view of what survives death. The experiment draws on the hypnotism research of the period, which Gurdjieff links to the school of Nancy.

    Surenian

    (Ch. V, p. 84), archimandrite. An archimandrite, a senior monk of the Armenian Church, and a distant relative of Pogossian in Echmiadzin, with whom Pogossian lodged and who received Gurdjieff kindly.

    Vasiliev and Yaroslev / Jaroslev

    (Ch. V, p. c. 86), engineers. Railway engineers in Tiflis: Yaroslev found Pogossian a job as a locksmith at the depot; Vasiliev hired Gurdjieff for the survey of the new line toward Kars, a period when the Russians were building railways across Transcaucasia.

    The book’s dates

    The book dates only a handful of events: the year 1888, which the young Gurdjieff letters on to a wedding shield (p. c. 65); Soloviev’s death in 1898 (p. 165); the gathering at Chardzhou on 2 January 1900 (p. c. 252); Pogossian’s turn to business in 1908 (p. c. 108); the move to Moscow near the end of 1913 (p. 270); the last sight of his father in 1916 (p. c. 45); the expedition of 1918 (p. c. 293); arrival in France in the summer of 1922 (p. 285); and New York on 8 April 1924 (p. c. 246). Everything else has to be placed by clues in the text: an ancient site discovered, a railway being built, a character whose name is on everyone’s lips.     Names, languages and transliteration

    Meetings with Remarkable Men moves through a dozen languages. Gurdjieff grew up among Armenians, Greeks, Kurds, Tartars and Russians, claims that as a young man he spoke eighteen languages, and wrote the book in Russian. The bards of his childhood sang in Turko-Tartar, the common tongue of the region; his companions are Armenian, Russian, Polish, Turkish, Persian and Georgian; and the places he passes through carry Turkish, Persian, Arabic, Armenian and Russian names, often several at once, as Alexandropol had been Gumri and Echmiadzin is also Vagharshapat. Every name therefore reaches the reader through Russian and then through a translator’s conventions.

    The two editions resolve this differently. The English edition anglicises: Bogachevsky, Soloviev, Tashkent, Yaroslev, Kara-Kirghiz, and distances in miles. The Adelphi edition follows its French source and a scholarly transliteration of Russian, with č for ch, š for sh, ž for zh, ë for yo, j for the consonant y, and an apostrophe for the Russian soft sign, as in Bogačevsky, Solovëv, Taškent, Jaroslev and Aleksandropol’; French habits show in accented endings and in ou for u (Nijeradzé, Sari Oglé, djouppé), and it keeps Russian forms such as the plural karakirgizy and distances in versts. Neither is wrong, obviously; they are two routes from the same Russian.

Behind the spellings lie cultures the book takes for granted: Armenian Christianity and its monasteries, the Assyrian churches, the Sufi orders of dervishes, the Islamic learning of Bukhara, the Russian imperial frontier with its garrisons, railways and exiles, and the nomad world of the Turkomans and Kirghiz.

    Glossary

    I have put together here the words from the book that a reader is unlikely to know, in alphabetical order and with much pedantry; click a word to read the entry. Where the English and Adelphi editions spell or render a word differently, both forms are given, English first. The chapter is the one where the word first appears; where Gurdjieff or another speaker explains a word, the entry says who, in paraphrase. Other definitions are general background or come from the Adelphi edition’s notes.

    Afridis

    (Ch. X, p. c. 236), ethnic group. A Pashtun tribe of the Tirah valley and the Khyber Pass, on what was then the north-west frontier of British India, famous as fighters and for their hold on the pass. Gurdjieff places their central settlement in the heart of Kafiristan, which cannot be right on two counts: Kafiristan lay well to the north, in the Hindu Kush, and the Afridis are Muslims, whereas the Kafirs were precisely the people who were not.

    Aisors / Aissors

    (Ch. V, p. 90), ethnic and religious group. The Assyrian Christians, known to Russians as Aisory, who speak a modern form of Aramaic. Gurdjieff describes them as descendants of the ancient Assyrians, driven from Mesopotamia into Persia by the Byzantines, mostly Nestorian, and scattered through Transcaucasia, north-western Persia, eastern Turkey and Asia Minor, some three million in all. The Byzantine persecution he mentions fell on the Church of the East after its teaching was condemned in 431, which is why it grew up under Persian rule. His figure of three million is his own and far above what historians estimate. He also recalls that half of them perished in the First World War, caught between Russia, Britain, the Kurds and the Persians: the massacres Assyrians remember as the Sayfo, “the sword”. Yelov was an Aisor.

    American canaries

    (Ch. VII, p. 137), trick. Sparrows that Gurdjieff caught, clipped to shape and coloured with aniline dyes, then sold in Samarkand as a rare American breed for two roubles each. The trick worked because the Sarts prized caged songbirds and canaries were costly imports; the new synthetic aniline dyes of the period gave colours no natural bird could have. He left town before rain could wash the colour off.

    Archimandrite

    (Ch. V, p. 83), title. A senior monk of the Eastern churches, ranking below a bishop and often the head of a large monastery; the word is Greek, “chief of the fold”. At Echmiadzin Pogossian lodged with a distant relative, the Archimandrite Surenian.

    Ashokh

    (Ch. II, p. 32), occupation. A bard. Gurdjieff explains that the name was used throughout Asia and the Balkans for local poets who composed, recited and sang poems, legends and tales, most of them illiterate but with prodigious memories, and he describes contests in which two bards improvised sung questions and answers for weeks. The word comes from the Arabic ‘āshiq, “lover”, and the tradition, the ashugh of the Armenians and the aşık of the Turks and Azerbaijanis, still survives. His father was a celebrated amateur under the name Adash.

    Aul

    (Ch. MQ), settlement. A village, especially a nomad encampment of felt tents, from a Turkic word for a settlement; the Adelphi edition glosses it simply as villages. Gurdjieff took his phonograph to the auls of wealthy Turkomans near Kizil-Arvat, where a machine that talked was a marvel.

    Baluchis

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 221), ethnic group. An Iranian-speaking, largely tribal people of Baluchistan, the arid country then divided between Persia, Afghanistan and British India and now mostly in Pakistan. The Afghan hermit was wounded at sixty in a rebellion of Afghans and Baluchis against a European power, which the book does not name.

    Bashlik

    (Ch. VII, p. 149), garment. A Caucasian hood of felt or wool with long ends that wrap round the neck, adopted as winter uniform by the Russian army. Gurdjieff notes that it can be put on so as to cover the face completely, which is how the guides blindfolded him and Soloviev on the way to the Sarmoung.

    Batchi

    (Ch. X, p. 230), occupation. Boy dancers of the Turkestan teahouses, dressed as girls to dance for male audiences, a practice known as bacha bazi, “boy play”, which often involved sexual exploitation. Gurdjieff describes Turkoman parties in a Merv teahouse and remarks acidly that the trade was authorised by local law and encouraged by the laws of the Russian Empire, which governed the region as a protectorate, just as Europe licensed prostitutes with yellow tickets.

    Bostani

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 136), garden. A vegetable garden, from the Persian bustān. Gurdjieff notes that in Turkestan the gardens were fenced off from one another and from the road by tall hedges of Jerusalem artichokes, a plant that grows thick and high in a season; hungry on his walk to Samarkand, he ate their tubers.

    Bourdiouk / burdiuk

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 224), object. A whole animal skin sewn up and blown full of air, used as a float; the word is Russian, from Turkic. Rafts on inflated skins have been used on the rivers of the Near East and Central Asia since antiquity. The Seekers made them from the hides of their animals to build the raft on which Karpenko was wounded.

    Cadet School

    (Ch. VI, p. c. 110), school. A military boarding school of the Russian Empire, the cadet corps, which took boys from about the age of ten and prepared them for the officer schools; most places went to the sons of nobles and officers. Yelov was cramming to enter one when Gurdjieff met him in Tiflis.

    Caracul

    (Ch. X, p. 229), livestock breed. A sheep of Central Asia named after the town of Karakul near Bukhara. The tightly curled pelts of its newborn lambs, sold as astrakhan or Persian lamb, were one of the region’s most valuable exports. The old Turkoman who helped plan the journey to Kafiristan owned a large flock, which marks him as a rich man.

    Caravanserai

    (Ch. V, p. 103), building. An inn on the caravan routes of the Near East and Central Asia: a walled courtyard for animals and goods, with rooms for travellers round it, from the Persian kārvān, “caravan”, and sarāy, “palace” or “inn”. Its keeper is the ustabash (Adelphi oustabash), from the Turkish usta, “master”, and baş, “head”; the word is blurred in the scanned English copy. Gurdjieff and Pogossian settle their bill with him in Smyrna before the sailors carry their belongings to the harbour.

    Catholicos / Katholikos

    (Ch. V, p. 83), title. The supreme patriarch of the Armenian Church, whose seat is Echmiadzin; the title, from the Greek for “universal”, is also borne by the heads of the Georgian Church and the Church of the East. Gurdjieff calls Echmiadzin, as the residence of the Catholicos of all Armenians, what Mecca is to Muslims and Jerusalem to Christians.

    Caucasian Tartars

    (Ch. II, p. 33), ethnic group. The Turkic Muslims of Transcaucasia, today’s Azerbaijanis. Russians called them Tatars or Tartars, a name they applied loosely to many Turkic peoples; the name Azerbaijani came into general use only in the 1930s. Both editions use the old spelling. Their Turko-Tartar language served as the common tongue of the bardic contests, and Gurdjieff and Pogossian later travelled disguised as Tartars (Ch. V), since two Armenians would have been in danger in Kurdistan.

    Chaikhana / čaikhané

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 157), building. A Central Asian teahouse, from the Persian chāy, “tea”, and khāna, “house”: a raised terrace or room where men drank green tea, talked, heard news and, in some places, watched dancers. Philos fetched boiling water from them for his master’s tea.

    Cherkesses

    (Ch. MQ, p. 275), ethnic group. The Circassians, a people of the north-western Caucasus. When the Russian conquest of the region ended in 1864, most of them were expelled or emigrated to the Ottoman Empire, one of the great forced migrations of the century. Crossing the mountains towards Sochi in 1918, Gurdjieff passed their fertile former lands, which he found still empty forty or fifty years after they left.

    Chourek / čurek

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 120), food. A flat bread, from the Turkic word for bread (çörek); Gurdjieff’s lunch at the foot of the pyramids when he overheard Skridlov and the Prince.

    Coin-catchers

    (Ch. VIII, p. c. 180), occupation. Boys who dived in the harbour of Constantinople for the coins that passengers threw from the steamers. Gurdjieff, in need of money, joined them; he explains that a coin sinks ever more slowly as it goes deeper, so that a diver who marks where it fell can catch it. Diving for an amber rosary that a passenger had dropped, he found it and returned it to its owner, Pasha N, which led to his friendship with Ekim Bey.

    Cornelian cherry

    (Ch. IX, p. 218), plant. The cornel (Cornus mas), a small tree of southern Europe and western Asia whose wood is exceptionally hard and dense and was prized from antiquity for spear shafts and tool handles. The Afghan hermit told the Seekers that it was the best wood for their purpose, which the book leaves unstated, and showed them where it grew.

    Dervish

    (Ch. IV, p. c. 73), religious figure. A member of a Sufi order, from the Persian darvīsh, “poor man”: Muslims who seek direct experience of God through poverty, discipline, music, breathing and movement, as in the turning of the Mevlevi. Dervishes are among Gurdjieff’s most frequent sources, companions and disguises in the book, and many scholars trace elements of his teaching, including the sacred dances, to Sufi practice.

    Djedjin

    (Ch. V, p. 99), textile. A flat-woven textile, related to the kilim, made across the Caucasus and Anatolia (elsewhere spelled jejim or cicim), used for bags, covers and hangings. In the Armenian priest’s story, the Russian prince who paid two hundred pounds to copy his old map took a plaster cast of it and wrapped the cast in a piece of old djedjin the priest gave him; the prince was Lubovedsky, as Gurdjieff discovers at the pyramids (Ch. VII). The word is illegible in the scanned English copy, so its English spelling could not be checked.

    Djuppay / djouppé

    (Ch. V, p. c. 78), garment. A costume. Gurdjieff explains that it is the special dress of the Armenian women of Erzerum, then a large Armenian community in eastern Anatolia; Pogossian’s mother, from Erzerum, embroidered its breast-pieces and belts in gold thread.

    Donga

    (Ch. X, p. c. 235), coin. A Bukharan coin, probably a form of tanga, the silver coin of the Emirate. Gurdjieff describes it as an irregularly cut piece of silver worth about half a French franc and the only coin of any value in Bukhara, so that any large sum had to be carried in bags, a real burden for travellers.

    Ernos

    (Ch. V, p. 90), community. A kind of guild or corporation, according to the Adelphi edition’s note; the word appears in the Ani letter for the Sarmoung community. It is not a known Armenian word, and like much in the letter it cannot be checked.

    Essenes

    (Ch. IV, p. c. 58), religious community. A Jewish ascetic movement of the late Second Temple period, described by Josephus and Philo; the Dead Sea Scrolls, found at Qumran from 1947, after Gurdjieff wrote, are usually linked to them. The movement disappears from history after the first century. Gurdjieff claims that an Essene brotherhood still existed, with its chief monastery near the Dead Sea, founded it is said twelve centuries before Christ, and that Jesus received his first initiation in it. The idea that Jesus was trained among the Essenes circulated widely in 19th-century esoteric writing; it has no support in the ancient sources. Bogachevsky rose to be assistant to its abbot.

    Fakir / fakhr

    (Ch. VIII, p. 192), religious figure. In European usage an Indian ascetic or wonder-worker; the word is the Arabic faqīr, “poor man”, the counterpart of the Persian darvīsh. Gurdjieff mocks the “Indian fakirs” of European stages and gives his own etymology: the word, more correctly fakhr, comes from a Turkoman word for “beggar” and has come to mean a swindler who exploits people’s piety, as against a lourie, who exploits their stupidity, the name also given to gypsies (Ch. IX, p. 214). The Arabic origin of the word makes the Turkoman derivation doubtful.

    Gilgamesh, legend of

    (Ch. II, p. 34), poem. The Babylonian epic of Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, whose eleventh tablet contains the story of the flood told by Ut-napishtim, survivor of the destruction of Shuruppak; the tablets were found at Nineveh, and George Smith announced the flood story in 1872. In the version Gurdjieff’s father sang, the flood is told in the twenty-first song. Gurdjieff later read the deciphered text and found it almost word for word the same as his father’s.

    Hatha Yoga

    (Ch. VIII, p. c. 184), practice. The branch of Indian yoga concerned with bodily postures, breathing and purification, which became popular in Europe and Russia in the early twentieth century through manuals such as those published under the name Yogi Ramacharaka. Gurdjieff, then its ardent follower, chewed every mouthful at length, until the Persian dervish showed him the harm of practices taken up without understanding.

    Imastun Brotherhood

    (Ch. II, p. c. 35), brotherhood. In a legend Gurdjieff’s father sang of the “flood before the Flood”, a learned caste spread over the earth, with its centre on the island of Haninn, where Greece now lies. Its brethren studied astrology and kept in touch over great distances by telepathy, through pythonesses. The Adelphi edition notes that imastun is old Armenian for “wise”, a title given to great figures such as King Solomon; it is indeed the Armenian word for wise. The brotherhood is known from no other source.

    Jacobites, Maronites and Gregorians

    (Ch. V, p. 90), religious communities. Three of the churches Gurdjieff lists among the minority of the Aisors, beside Catholics and the Nestorian majority: the Jacobites, the Syriac Orthodox Church, named after the sixth-century bishop Jacob Baradaeus; the Maronites, a Syriac church centred in Lebanon and in communion with Rome; and the Gregorians, the usual Russian name for the Armenian Church, after St Gregory the Illuminator, who converted Armenia.

    Kafirs

    (Ch. VII, p. 134), ethnic group. “Unbelievers”, the Muslim name for the peoples of the high Hindu Kush who kept their own polytheistic religion. Their land, Kafiristan, was closed to outsiders until the Emir of Afghanistan conquered and forcibly converted it in 1895–96, renaming it Nuristan, “land of light”. It is also the setting of Kipling’s story The Man Who Would Be King. Gurdjieff does not say when he went there; if before 1896, it was still pagan.

    Kara-Kirghiz / karakirgizy

    (Ch. VII, p. 149), ethnic group. The Kyrgyz. In the 19th century Russians called the Kazakhs “Kirghiz” and so named the Kyrgyz of the Tian Shan and Pamirs “Kara-Kirghiz”, “black Kirghiz”; the Adelphi edition keeps the Russian plural. Four of them met Gurdjieff and Soloviev at Yeni-Hissar and guided them to the Sarmoung monastery.

    Kastousilia / kastusilia

    (Ch. II, p. c. 38), practice. A game played by Gurdjieff’s father and Dean Borsh. Gurdjieff describes it as one of them suddenly asking an apparently absurd question and the other answering calmly, seriously and plausibly, as when Borsh asked where God was just now and the father answered that he was in Sari Kamish, making ladders. He calls it an original means of developing the mind and thought the word came from ancient Assyrian; no such word is known.

    Keesiak / kiziak

    (Ch. V, p. 95), fuel. Dried dung, shaped into cakes and dried in the sun, the main fuel of the treeless steppes and highlands; the Russian word comes from Turkic. A Kurdish girl gathering it in the fields rescued Gurdjieff and Pogossian from the sheepdogs.

    Kishlak

    (Ch. VII, p. 147), settlement. In Central Asia, a village of settled people, from a Turkic word for winter quarters (qishlaq). Gurdjieff writes it with a capital, as the name of a place near New Bukhara where the old man of the Sarmoung was staying; it may stand for any village. The Adelphi edition has a different, unidentified name at this point.

    Kobzir

    (Ch. X, p. c. 235), vessel. A raft. The Adelphi edition notes that it is a wooden raft fixed on burdiuks, skins blown full of air: the same kind of craft, known on the Tigris as a kelek, that the Seekers built in the Himalaya. Gurdjieff and Skridlov hired one beyond Kerki to go on up the Amu Darya.

    Kovurma

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 212), food. Meat fried in its own fat and sealed in fat to keep for months, from a Turkic word for “fried”. The Seekers carried it in bags made of sheep’s stomachs and shared it with Russian surveyors in the Pamirs.

    Kurds

    (Ch. IV, p. c. 65), ethnic group. An Iranian-speaking people of the mountains where Turkey, Persia and Mesopotamia meet, largely Muslim and then mostly pastoral and tribal. In the 1890s the Ottoman government armed Kurdish tribes as irregular cavalry and used them against the Armenians, which is why Gurdjieff and Pogossian, travelling in eastern Anatolia, feared the “bands of Kurds” and disguised themselves. In the book Kurds also appear as ferrymen, shepherds, the owners of fierce sheepdogs and the girl who rescued them.

    Kvass / kvas

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 135), drink. A mildly fermented Russian drink made from rye bread, the everyday drink of the Russian poor; Gurdjieff’s landlady in New Bukhara sold it to the Russian settlers.

    Lamaists

    (Ch. X, p. 238), religious community. Tibetan and Mongolian Buddhists, so called by Europeans after their lamas; Kalmyks and Buryats in the Russian Empire were among them. Former Lamaists were among the brethren of the World Brotherhood, with Christians, Jews, Muslims, Buddhists and one Shamanist.

    Merkhavat

    (Ch. V, p. 90), book. An old book in which Gurdjieff and Pogossian had several times met the word “Sarmoung” before finding it again in the Ani letter. No such book is known. The name recalls merkavah, the “chariot” of Ezekiel’s vision and of early Jewish mysticism, but that may be coincidence.

    Mollavaly

    (Ch. III), place and wine. A small place south of Kars known for a special wine, according to the Adelphi edition’s note. Dean Borsh uses its must in a warning: a single drop of alcohol turns the must to vinegar, and self-indulgence before maturity spoils a young man in the same way.

    Molokans

    (Ch. V, p. 84), religious sect. Russian religious dissenters who rejected icons, priests and the Orthodox liturgy; the name, from moloko, “milk”, was given because they drank milk during Orthodox fasts. Many were exiled or resettled in Transcaucasia from the 1830s, where their villages were known for sobriety and good farming. Gurdjieff put his belongings on their wagon on the pilgrimage to Echmiadzin.

    Monopsyche Brotherhood / Monopsychist brethren

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 132), brotherhood. A brotherhood whose experiments with music Vitvitskaia witnessed on an expedition to the Pamirs and Afghanistan, as she tells in her own account. The name, from the Greek for “one soul”, suggests a doctrine of a single shared soul, but the book says nothing more, and it is known from no other source.

    Mueller / Müller

    (Ch. VIII, p. c. 184), system. The gymnastics of J. P. Müller, whose My System (1904), a quarter of an hour of daily exercises, was a best-seller across Europe and Russia before the First World War. Gurdjieff practised it twice a day until the Persian dervish told him that such exercises develop only the outer muscles. Both editions call Müller a Swede; he was Danish.

    Nestorians

    (Ch. V, p. 91), religious community. The Church of the East, named by its opponents after Nestorius, the 5th-century patriarch of Constantinople whose teaching on the two natures of Christ was condemned at the Council of Ephesus in 431. Gurdjieff says most Aisors belong to it and do not recognise the divinity of Christ. That is inaccurate: the Church of the East affirms Christ’s divinity, but distinguishes his divine and human natures more sharply than the churches that condemned it.

    Phalanga / tarantula

    (Ch. V, p. c. 96), creature. The big yellow creature that bit Pogossian on the road to Kurdistan. A phalanga is the Russian name for a solifuge or camel spider, a large, fast arachnid of the steppes. The English edition calls it a phalanga and adds that it is a kind of tarantula; the Adelphi edition calls it simply a tarantula. Both were greatly feared in the region, though solifuges have no venom and their bite, while painful, is not dangerous in itself.

    Poiadji

    (Ch. V, p. c. 78), occupation. A dyer, from the Turkish boyacı. Gurdjieff notes that one can always be recognised by arms blue to the elbow from indigo that never washes off. It was Pogossian’s father’s trade.

    Pokhand

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 221), food. A flour. Gurdjieff explains that it is made from roasted barley and makes the tastiest of cakes; flour of roasted barley, like the tsampa of Tibet, is a staple of high mountain peoples because it needs no further cooking. The Seekers shared the cakes with the Afghan hermit.

    Pre-sand Egypt / map of ancient Egypt

    (Ch. V, p. 99), map. The old map owned by the Armenian priest of town N, which Gurdjieff copied in secret and carried to the pyramids to study the Sphinx: a map of Egypt before the desert covered it. The Adelphi edition calls it simply a map of ancient Egypt. The idea is not pure fantasy: the Sahara was green and inhabited in a humid phase that ended some five thousand years ago. Gurdjieff, however, meant something grander, in keeping with his belief, set out in Beelzebub’s Tales, that the Sphinx and the pyramids belong to a civilisation far older than conventional history allows.

    Pythonesses

    (Ch. II, p. c. 35), mediums. In the legend of the Imastun Brotherhood, women who in a trance received the brethren’s telepathic reports and wrote them down, in one of four agreed directions according to where each report came from. The name comes from the Pythia, the priestess of Apollo at Delphi who prophesied in a trance.

    Sarmoung / Sarmung

    (Ch. V, p. 90), esoteric school. Gurdjieff describes it as a famous esoteric school said to have been founded in Babylon about 2500 BC and known to have existed in Mesopotamia until the sixth or seventh century. It is known only from Gurdjieff. His pupil J. G. Bennett proposed Persian etymologies for the name and looked for it among the Sufis of Central Asia, and later writers have claimed contact with it, but there is no independent evidence, and most scholars regard it as a literary invention.

    Sarts

    (Ch. VII, p. 121), ethnic group. The settled, town-dwelling people of Turkestan, as distinct from the nomads, whether they spoke a Turkic language or Persian; the Russian authorities used the term widely, and it was dropped in the 1920s when the Soviet state created the Uzbek nationality. Gurdjieff notes their fondness for canaries and songbirds, which made them buyers of his dyed sparrows.

    Seekers of Truth

    (Ch. IX, p. 208), circle. Gurdjieff’s name for his group of companions, among them engineers, doctors and an archaeologist, who pooled their research and travelled together. The group is known only from his own writings and talks; Ouspensky records him mentioning it in Moscow, but none of its members can be traced independently.

    Seid / sëid

    (Ch. X, p. 230), title. A descendant of the Prophet Muhammad (Arabic sayyid), honoured throughout the Muslim world and often a wandering holy man. Gurdjieff chose it as his disguise for Kafiristan, since it would earn him hospitality and alms without questions.

    Shamanist

    (Ch. X, p. 238), religious community. A follower of the shamanic religions of Siberia and Central Asia, in which a shaman deals with the spirits in a trance on behalf of the community. One former Shamanist was among the brethren of the World Brotherhood.

    Tadzhiks / Tajiks

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 156), ethnic group. The Persian-speaking settled people of Central Asia, in Bukhara and Samarkand and in the mountains of the upper Amu Darya; under Russian rule they were usually counted among the Sarts. The old man of the Sarmoung was talking about them over tea in Kabul when he broke off to address the Prince.

    Tamil

    (Ch. VII, p. c. 156), ethnic group. A people of southern India and northern Ceylon who speak Tamil, a Dravidian language with a literature some two thousand years old. The old man of the Sarmoung sat in the Aga Khan’s house in Kabul as an old Tamil, in a costume out of place there; the book does not say whether he was one or only dressed as one.

    Tarekh

    (Ch. V, p. c. 96), food. A very salty fish, much prized in the region and caught only in Lake Van, according to the Adelphi edition’s note. It is the pearl mullet of Lake Van (Alburnus tarichi, locally tarek), the only fish that lives in the lake’s soda water, which is caught when it runs up the rivers to spawn and preserved in salt. Pogossian was eating it when he was bitten.

    Tekinian / Tekke

    (Ch. X, p. c. 228), ethnic group. A large Turkmen tribe of the Akhal oasis and Merv, the last to resist the Russian conquest of Central Asia, at Geok Tepe in 1881. Gurdjieff’s Greek friend Vasiliaki wore Tekke dress, and the teahouses of New Merv are Tekke teahouses.

    Theosophist

    (Ch. IX, p. 209), adherent. A member of the Theosophical Society, founded in New York in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky and Henry Steel Olcott, which taught a wisdom said to be kept by hidden masters in the East and drew many wealthy patrons. Gurdjieff mocks expeditions paid for by “a banker’s widow, an ardent Theosophist”, whose members hire porters, whereas a real traveller packs his own baggage.

    Top-bashi

    (Ch. IX, p. c. 221), rank. Chief of artillery, as Gurdjieff explains, from the Turkish and Persian top, “cannon”, and bāshī, “head”; the former rank of the Afghan hermit under the Emir’s grandfather.

    Turkomans

    (Ch. IX, p. 215), ethnic group. The Turkmen, a Turkic people of the Transcaspian steppe and deserts, until the Russian conquest of the 1880s nomads, horse-breeders and feared raiders. In the book they appear as the owners of carpets, camels and caracul flocks, and as Gurdjieff’s audience for the phonograph.

    Verst

    (Ch. IV), unit. A Russian measure of distance, 1.07 kilometres. The English edition converts versts to miles where the Adelphi edition keeps them, so that the walk from town P to Samarkand is about seventy miles in one and a hundred versts in the other.

    Wolf tickets

    (Ch. VII, p. 142), document. A “wolf ticket” (volchiy bilet) was an internal passport or certificate marking its holder as unreliable, which barred him from state employment and from living in the capitals. Gurdjieff uses the term for the exiles from Russia living under police surveillance in New Bukhara, a frontier town where the empire sent people it did not want at home.

    World Brotherhood / Universal Brotherhood

    (Ch. X, p. c. 238), brotherhood. The order of Father Giovanni’s monastery. Father Giovanni explains that any man could enter it whatever his former religion, and that it had four monasteries, one of them in the Pamirs, one in Tibet and one in India. A brotherhood above all creeds was a favourite idea of the period, promoted for instance by the Theosophical Society, whose first object was “universal brotherhood”; nothing is known of this one outside the book. The Adelphi edition calls it the Universal Brotherhood.

    Yellow tickets

    (Ch. X, p. 230), document. In the Russian Empire a registered prostitute surrendered her internal passport and received a yellow card instead, which subjected her to police and medical control and made it hard to leave the trade. Gurdjieff mentions it to compare the boy dancers of the Merv teahouses with the licensed prostitution of Europe.

    Yezidis / Yazidis

    (Ch. IV, p. c. 65), religious community. A religious community, Kurdish-speaking, whose faith draws on ancient Iranian religion, Islamic Sufism and Christianity. Gurdjieff describes them as a sect living in Transcaucasia, mainly near Mount Ararat, sometimes called devil-worshippers. The name came from their neighbours: the Yezidis venerate Melek Taus, the Peacock Angel, chief of the seven angels to whom God entrusted the world. Muslims and Christians identified him with Satan, the angel who refused to bow to Adam; for the Yezidis he was never evil, and they avoid even the word for Satan. The label was used to justify centuries of persecution. Gurdjieff watched a Yezidi boy unable to leave a circle drawn round him on the ground, a belief about Yezidis that travellers in the region often reported.

    Part IIAnalysis and miscellanea

    G. I. Gurdjieff

    Gurdjieff was born in Alexandropol, in Russian Armenia, to a Greek father and an Armenian mother; the year is uncertain, Gurdjieff himself claiming he was born in 1866, his passport in 1877. For the decades before he appeared in Moscow as a teacher, around 1912, this book is almost the only source. In Russia he gathered pupils, among them the writer P. D. Ouspensky, and taught what came to be called the Fourth Way: a method of self-observation and “self-remembering” for people living ordinary lives, rather than in monasteries, joined to sacred dances he called movements and music written ad hoc.

    Revolution, civil war and world war drove him through the Caucasus, Constantinople and Berlin to France, where in 1922 he founded the Institute for the Harmonious Development of Man at the Prieuré near Fontainebleau. After a near-fatal car accident in 1924 he turned to writing. He died in Paris in 1949. To his followers he was a master; to his detractors, a charlatan; he apparently had spirit enough to enjoy provoking both views.

    Gurdjieff seen by others

    “Of himself, G. spoke little.”

    P. D. Ouspensky, In Search of the Miraculous, Ch. I (my translation from the Italian)

    Those who knew Gurdjieff saw only his later life. Thomas de Hartmann, the composer, and his wife Olga followed him from Essentuki in 1918, and their memoir, Our Life with Mr. Gurdjieff (1964; enlarged edition 1992), tells of the departure under cover of a scientific expedition, the weeks held up at Maikop, in southern Russia, the crossing of the mountains to Sochi and the years in Tiflis, Constantinople and Berlin that led to the Prieuré, in outline as “The Material Question” tells them. P. D. Ouspensky, who was with him in Moscow, Petrograd and Essentuki from 1915 to 1918, describes those years in In Search of the Miraculous (1949; in Italian Frammenti di un insegnamento sconosciuto). Neither saw anything of his life before 1912.

    Ouspensky’s opening chapter is the earliest description of Gurdjieff by an outsider. The two met in Moscow after Easter 1915, in a small café far from the centre: a man of Oriental type, introduced as “a Greek from the Caucasus”, who spoke poor Russian with a strong Caucasian accent, and whose ballet, The Struggle of the Magicians, the Moscow press had announced a few months earlier as the work of “a Hindu”. He mentioned his travels in the East once or twice, and Ouspensky never found out where exactly he had been. Asked how he had learned what he knew, Gurdjieff said he had not been alone: there were specialists of every kind among them, each studying by the methods of his own science, and when they met they shared their results. Some of his companions, he said, had died, some were continuing their work, and some were “in seclusion”. Here, in a few lines, are the Seekers of Truth of Meetings, and the companion “in seclusion” recalls Prince Lubovedsky ending his days in the Olman monastery.

    Three remarks in the chapter touch the book’s geography. Knowledge, Gurdjieff said, had long ago been divided, so that India kept “philosophy”, Egypt “theory”, and the region of Persia, Mesopotamia and Turkestan “practice”, which is precisely where Meetings concentrates its search. He had several times seen sacred dances in ancient temples, and had put some of them into his ballet. And as examples of objective art he named the Great Sphinx, which he studies with the priest’s map in Chapter V, and a statue his party had found in the desert at the foot of the Hindu Kush, on whose body a whole cosmological system was inscribed; that statue does not appear in Meetings.

    Later writers divide over how to read the book. J. G. Bennett, in Gurdjieff: Making a New World (1973) and The Masters of Wisdom (1977), looked for Gurdjieff’s sources and read the remarkable men as partly symbolic. James Webb, in The Harmonious Circle (1980), was sceptical of it as a record. James Moore, in Gurdjieff: The Anatomy of a Myth (1991), called the Sarmoung episode a litmus test that separates literal readers from those who prefer allegory; Mark Sedgwick, a historian of Sufism, holds the brotherhood to be entirely imaginary; and the literary scholar Paul Beekman Taylor reads the whole book as parable. Most recently, Michael Benham (2025) has argued from documents for a birth in 1877.

    The book in Gurdjieff’s work

    The book opens with an Introduction, written at the Prieuré a month after Gurdjieff finished the first series. Most of it is given over to the speech of an elderly Persian on the emptiness of modern literature and journalism. Nine chapters follow, each named after a person: his father, the ashokh; his first tutor, Dean Borsh; Bogachevsky; Captain Pogossian, the “Mr X” of the chapter’s title; Abram Yelov; Prince Yuri Lubovedsky, whose long chapter contains the stories of Vitvitskaia and of Soloviev and his death; Ekim Bey; Piotr Karpenko; and Professor Skridlov. The portraits move roughly from childhood in Kars and Alexandropol to the journeys of the Seekers of Truth and on to the years of the First World War, though the chronology is loose and the narrative often doubles back. A final chapter, “The Material Question”, is the account Gurdjieff gave in New York in 1924 of how he had financed his Institute. In the note that opens the book, his pupils Jeanne de Salzmann and Henri Tracol explain that it was added to provide a definitive answer to the material question, and that it had not originally been meant to appear.

    The book is the second series of All and Everything, the three-part work Gurdjieff planned as the written form of his teaching. The first series, Beelzebub’s Tales to His Grandson (1950), is a vast allegorical epic in which a being from another world surveys human history. The second, the Meetings, is the only one written as autobiography. The third, Life Is Real Only Then, When “I Am”, was left unfinished (see “Teaching by absence”). Gurdjieff published only Beelzebub in his lifetime, apart from a short pamphlet of 1933, The Herald of Coming Good, which he later withdrew. Of the three series, Meetings is the easiest to read, and the usual way into the rest, despite Gurdjieff's own raccomandation not to read his work in a different order than the one he defined.

    The two editions

    The English edition is A. R. Orage’s translation, revised and reworked against Gurdjieff’s Russian manuscript; the Adelphi edition, translated by Gisèle Bartoli, was made at the rights-holders’ wish from the French. Therefore, as I have already mentioned, the two versions descend from the Russian by different routes, and neither is the original. In substance they are the same book: the same ten chapters and closing account of the Institute’s finances, “The Material Question”, the same sub-sections on Vitvitskaia and Soloviev, and chapters of matching length. The prefaces differ, since the Adelphi edition prints the French edition’s biographical introduction instead of the English Foreword. The texts part company in particulars. The river leading to the 1898 desert crossing is the “Sharakshan”, unknown to geography, where the Adelphi edition has the Charchan Darya, the Qarqan river of the Tarim basin. Prince Lubovedsky’s expedition with the monk “A” goes up the Ganges, where the Adelphi edition says the Indus. The Ashkhabad workshop earns fifty thousand roubles in three and a half months, against about seven thousand five hundred in three months in the Adelphi. Smaller discrepancies: ten days to Tabriz against nine; five days from Baku to Krasnovodsk against twenty-four hours; about two months to Sochi against some weeks; Baron X against Baron F; a phalanga, glossed as a kind of tarantula, against a plain tarantula; a journey on the Volga where the Adelphi specifies a sleigh; reaching the Red Sea where the Adelphi crosses it. One passage of the Ekim Bey chapter, on the journey through Margelan, is known here only from the Adelphi edition, because the page is missing from the scanned English copy. Differences of spelling are listed in the glossary and the list of characters. Without penetrating the Russian manuscript it is impossible to say which reading is Gurdjieff’s.

    Precision and haze

    Read with the map open, Meetings turns out to be precise and vague in a pattern. Of the 111 places on the map, sixteen are places where Gurdjieff’s presence is attested outside the book, ten are hidden or disguised beyond any checking, eight sit awkwardly with geography or history, and the rest are simply plausible. The documented places cluster at the two ends of the life: the Alexandropol, Kars and Tiflis of his youth, and the cities of the flight west after 1917, from Essentuki and Maikop to Berlin and the Prieuré. “The Material Question”, the most worldly chapter, has thirteen documented places and not one doubtful one. The hidden places are of another kind. Several stand at a threshold: the town “N” of the priest with the old map, the ruins of Yeni-Hissar where the guides wait to lead the way to the Sarmoung, the village of the Persian dervish. The rest belong to the remotest journeys, an avalanche, a raft in unmapped gorges, the source of the Nile. And every monastery in which something essential is taught, the Sarmoung, the Olman monastery, the house of the World Brotherhood and the chief monastery of the Essenes, is one the map cannot place with any confidence.

    The pattern is partly of my own making, of course: a monastery no one has ever found was bound to get a sceptical label. Still, it holds. The closer the book comes to the knowledge it is looking for, the further it moves from any place a reader could visit. Railway connections, the takings of a workshop and the streets of Tiflis are given with a merchant’s exactness; the road to the Sarmoung is travelled with a hood over the face, and Gurdjieff swears never to reveal it.

    Time behaves in the same way. The book’s dates are few, and they too thicken at the ends: from 1913 onward, when others were watching, they agree with the record (see “The book’s dates”). Before 1900 they will not hold together. Between the railway survey of 1894 and the meeting at Chardzhou in January 1900 the book fits Egypt, Abyssinia and Babylon, a Siberian expedition, Mecca, Bukhara and the Sarmoung, the Gobi and the Transcaspian workshops, while its own clues push some of these past 1898 or even 1901, and no choice of birth year rescues the arithmetic. To me the years of travel read as a single dense season of apprenticeship, arranged for the story and never kept as a diary.

    It would be easy to make this a matter of truth and falsehood, and I think it would be a mistake. The geography of Meetings behaves like that of a pilgrim’s tale, in which the road is real up to the threshold and the sanctuary lies on no map. Gurdjieff gave other dates elsewhere, three stray bullets in Crete in 1896, in Tibet in 1902 and near the Chiatura tunnel at the end of 1904, and the book mentions none of them; Tibet itself appears in it only in passing. I suspect the haze is there less to guard a secret itinerary than to steer the reader’s attention towards what was learnt, and away from where.

    Masters without mysticism

    Meetings was written in the 1920s for readers formed by the occult revival of the previous half-century: séances and table-turning, hypnotism, Indian fakirs on European stages, manuals of Hatha Yoga, and the Theosophical Society, whose founder Helena Blavatsky claimed to be taught by masters, the Mahatmas, living in Tibet. Their letters to the journalist A. P. Sinnett were published in 1923, the year after Gurdjieff settled at the Prieuré. The book helps itself to the whole repertoire, and what it does with each piece is telling.

    Almost every time, the fashion is a door that opens onto nothing, or is closed with a joke. The search begins in grief: the boy, mourning his favourite sister, hears the officers of Kars argue over table-turning and spirits (Ch. IV), and Prince Lubovedsky turns to spiritualism after the death of his young wife, to be disappointed in his turn (Ch. VII). The Persian dervish laughs at the young Gurdjieff’s Hatha Yoga, his endless chewing and his breathing exercises (Ch. VIII). The fakirs of the European stage are dismissed, and the word itself is said to mean a swindler who trades on piety (Ch. IX). Baron X, “an ardent occultist”, dies in an avalanche on the way from the Pamirs to India. Expeditions paid for by “a banker’s widow, an ardent Theosophist” are set against real travel, in which a man carries his own pack. And in “The Material Question” Gurdjieff mocks the rumour that his Institute is funded by an occult centre in India.

    Against this he sets a different cast. The Seekers of Truth are professionals, a mining engineer, doctors, an archaeologist, an astronomer, a philologist who sells books, and they work as Gurdjieff described them to Ouspensky, each by the methods of his own science. The teachers, when they appear, are old men met in a village beyond Tabriz, among the dervishes of Bukhara, in a house in Kabul. No letter falls from the ceiling. The marvels the book does admit, a stranger who knows the Prince’s childhood name, a boy unable to step out of a circle drawn on the ground, are reported plainly and left unexplained.

    Yet the structure of the occult tale survives. There is a brotherhood older than history, an envoy who arrives unasked and knows one’s childhood name, a monastery reached blindfold, and an order above all creeds whose very name echoes the Theosophists’ first object, universal brotherhood. What Gurdjieff changes is the price of admission. In the Theosophical story the masters choose their correspondents and teach by letter; in Meetings the seeker earns his way by skill, money and endurance, and arrives as something like a colleague. One could say that the hidden masters have been moved from the astral plane to the caravan road. Gurdjieff does not quarrel with the Theosophists over whether such men exist; he quarrels over how one gets to them.

    Gurdjieff, splendide mendax?

    The book presents itself as autobiography and often reads as a teaching story, and its narrator is, among other things, a cheerful liar.

    The title borrows the motto printed under Gulliver’s portrait in Swift’s Travels, splendide mendax, “splendidly lying”, and the parallel is close. Both books are first-person accounts of travels among strange peoples by a narrator who insists on his own candour, and both use those peoples to hold a mirror up to modern civilisation. Both tell the truth by way of invention. The difference is that Swift’s book declares itself a fiction and its lie is satire, whereas Gurdjieff’s asks to be read as a life, his “as it were, autobiography” (p. c. 30), and its inventions are meant to teach.

    The narrator is a trickster in the old sense. In Samarkand he clips sparrows, dyes them and sells them as American canaries, leaving town before the rain can wash them (Ch. VII); along the Transcaspian railway he sells corsets, mends anything that can be mended and deals in whatever pays, and “The Material Question” is a catalogue of such enterprises told with evident relish. His model, quoted from the first pages, is Mullah Nassr Eddin, the wise fool of the Near East whose jokes hide their wisdom. The book even supplies a training for its reader. In the game his father and Dean Borsh call kastousilia, one asks an absurd question and the other answers calmly, seriously and plausibly: asked where God is just now, the father replies that he is in Sari Kamish, making ladders (Ch. II). It is a fair description of how Meetings answers many of the questions put to it.

    Those who met him saw the same thing. Ouspensky felt from the first evening that Gurdjieff was playing a part, dropping words to steer his listener’s thoughts, and caught him in an open bluff: the “very expensive apartments” he spoke of turned out to be an empty flat meant for schoolteachers. That evening he also heard “Glimpses of Truth”, a story read as the work of an absent pupil, and learned later that two pupils in the room had written it, on Gurdjieff’s idea, under an invented name: an early case of his teaching presented through a fictitious first-person narrative.

    The longest of the book’s tall tales is the crossing of the desert in 1898 (Ch. VII). After a month in which each Seeker works on the problem alone, they draw lots to deliver their reports. Karpenko proposes to feed a flock of sheep and goats on a mixture three-quarters sand and to slaughter them along the way, each animal carrying two skins of water; Dr Sari-Ogli, who has found the air clear of sand a few metres up during a storm, proposes crossing on stilts; Yelov crowns it by harnessing the sheep to carry stilts and travellers alike, who can learn languages on the way, lounging like a pasha in his harem; Pogossian adds pack-saddles to be burned as firewood, and the astronomer Dashtamirov steers by the stars. The setting is real enough, since the cities buried in the sands of the Tarim basin were news in those very years: Sven Hedin found Dandan-Uiliq near Keriya in 1896, and Aurel Stein excavated it in 1900–01. The plan is a comedy of ingenuity, and Gurdjieff himself remarks that after the reports the dangers of the desert seemed deliberately exaggerated. It also frames Soloviev’s death, which is told plainly and without any trick at all.

    Gurdjieff held that knowledge could be carried by deliberate inexactitudes, planted in a work for a prepared reader to decode, and he told Ouspensky that telling the truth is the hardest thing in the world. A teaching lie and an ordinary one look alike from outside, and the book never says which is which; the refusal is part of the method. The Sufi tradition the book draws on has a name for something close to it, the “path of blame”, malamatiyya, of those who hide their virtue under conduct that invites reproach. The reader is not asked to believe in the stilts, only to notice what the stilts are for.

    What the book sets out to teach

    Gurdjieff was explicit about his intentions, if not about his methods. His three series were to do three things in turn: the first, Beelzebub’s Tales, to destroy the reader’s inherited beliefs; the second, this book, “to acquaint the reader with the material required for a new creation”, in the words of the programme printed at the head of Beelzebub; the third, to help a true picture of the real world arise in its place. In the Introduction to Meetings he says how the material will travel: through tales about the men he has met, as “preparatory constructive material” (p. c. 30). He opens with an ancient saying about keeping intact both the wolf and the sheep entrusted to one’s care, the body and the feelings under the charge of reason, which only a man who has undergone “conscious labours and voluntary sufferings” can do (pp. 3–4). That phrase is as near as the book comes to a thesis.

    The portraits then work as a curriculum, each teacher supplying one lesson. His father hardens the child against disgust and fear, slipping frogs and worms into his bed and sending him at dawn to wash in cold spring water, and remains in old age a man who “never identified himself with anything” (pp. 44–47); his sayings on pp. 46–47 teach by paradox. Dean Borsh sets out ten principles of education, among them hope of reward only for merit, remorse for cruelty to animals, fearlessness towards devils, snakes and mice, and contentment with what one has (p. 56), and warns that indulgence before maturity turns a youth to vinegar like must spoiled by alcohol (p. 54). Bogachevsky distinguishes objective morality, founded on a conscience that is the same everywhere, from the subjective morality of local convention (pp. c. 72–76). Pogossian proves that no conscious labour is ever wasted (p. 107). The Persian dervish shows Gurdjieff that practices borrowed without understanding, his careful chewing and his yogic breathing, do more harm than good (pp. c. 186–189). Father Giovanni speaks of faith, love and hope as fruits of understanding rather than of instruction (p. 239). And “The Material Question” turns money-making into a discipline of attention and ingenuity in the service of an aim. If I had to put the common thread in one line: know yourself by effort, not by doctrine; trust conscience over custom; distrust what you have only been told; look in company; and let resourcefulness do what piety cannot.

    Nothing in this education is received passively. The father, the dean and the dervish do not hand on a doctrine; they hand on a way of testing things, which may be why so much of the teaching is never actually stated.

    Teaching by absence

    “The details of everything in this monastery, what it represented, and what was done there and how, I shall perhaps recount at some time in a special book.”

    Ch. VII

    Meetings repeatedly defers its most important material to books that were never written. Three teachings are promised to the third series, each as a chapter with a title of its own: the Persian dervish’s explanations of nutrition and the body, in “The physical body of man, its needs according to law, and possibilities of manifestation” (Ch. VIII, p. c. 190); the sermon of the old man who healed Vitvitskaia, Skridlov and Yelov in Central Asia, in “The astral body of man, its needs and possibilities of manifestation according to law” (Ch. IX, p. c. 222); and Father Giovanni’s words on the soul, in “The divine body of man, and its needs and possible manifestations according to law”, which Gurdjieff presents as completing the other two (Ch. X, p. c. 242). He also says he may one day describe the Sarmoung monastery “in a special book” (Ch. VII, p. c. 160), and that he means to write “an entire book” about his years of money-making (The Material Question, p. c. 268). The Introduction goes further, speaking of ideas to be elucidated “in the last two series”, as if a fourth were planned, and recalls that the whole work was first conceived as thirty-six books.

    None of these was written. The third series, published after his death as Life Is Real Only Then, When “I Am”, contains a prologue, an introduction, five talks given in New York in 1930–31 and an unfinished essay, “The Outer and Inner World of Man”; its editors call it no more than a fragmentary and preliminary draft, and its foreword records that Gurdjieff stopped writing abruptly on 2 April 1935 and never resumed. The doctrine behind the three promised chapters, that a man may come to possess a physical, an astral and a divine body, is set out at length in Beelzebub’s Tales, and Ouspensky reports Gurdjieff teaching it in 1915–17 in a four-body version. What is missing is the part that belongs to Meetings itself: the actual words of the dervish, the old man and Father Giovanni, which the book announces and withholds.

    Whether Gurdjieff ran out of time or left the gap on purpose, the absence fits everything else in the book. Places are hidden behind initials; the way to the Sarmoung is sworn to secrecy; the old man who heals Vitvitskaia, Skridlov and Yelov preaches, and the reader is told only that he preached; the Persian dervish explains the care of the body to Ekim Bey, and the explanation is postponed to another book. Even the questions of the Introduction, about the soul, free will and the occult, receive no answer but stories.

    Father Giovanni gives the reason, if a reason is wanted. Faith, he tells Skridlov, cannot be given to man; it arises from understanding, and understanding from what a man has learned and lived for himself. A book that believed this could not hand over its teaching without contradicting itself. It can only bring the reader to the edge of a teaching and leave him there, with the suspicion that the missing chapters would not have helped. Read like this, Meetings is not so much a record of what Gurdjieff found as an apprenticeship in looking. The portraits show how each man came to his question; the silences give the question back to the reader.

    Music

    Mostly between 1925 and 1927, at the Prieuré, Gurdjieff and Thomas de Hartmann wrote down some three hundred pieces for piano: Gurdjieff played or whistled melodies, many of which he said he had heard on his travels, and de Hartmann harmonised them. Whether they are transcriptions of folk music or Gurdjieff’s own compositions in folk style is still debated. Several carry the names of peoples and places in the book. The titles below are those of the recording Music for the Piano, Vol. 1: Asian Songs and Rhythms (Wergo, 1998) and of Music of Georges I. Gurdjieff (ECM, 2011), on which the Gurdjieff Folk Instruments Ensemble under Levon Eskenian plays the pieces on the duduk, kamancha, tar and other instruments of the region.

    • Song of the Aïsors and Assyrian Women Mourners: the Aisors, Yelov’s people, and the book’s account of their losses in the First World War (Ch. V–VI).
    • Kurd Shepherd Melody, Kurd Shepherd’s Dance, Atarnakh, Kurd Song and Kurd Melody from Isfahan: the Kurdish country crossed with Pogossian, the sheepdogs and the girl gathering dung for fuel (Ch. V), and Persia.
    • Armenian Melody, Armenian Song and Duduki, after the duduk, the Armenian double-reed pipe: Alexandropol, Echmiadzin and Ani.
    • Song of the Molokans: the Russian dissenters in whose wagon Gurdjieff stowed his belongings on the pilgrimage to Echmiadzin (p. 84), and Molokans Street in Tiflis, where he later lived (p. c. 112).
    • Greek Melody, Greek Song and Ancient Greek Dance: his father’s Greek family (Ch. II).
    • Persian Song and Persian Dance: Persia with Ekim Bey and the Persian dervish (Ch. VIII).
    • Afghan Melody: the journey to Kafiristan (Ch. X).
    • Sayyid Chant and Dance, several numbers: on the road to Kafiristan Gurdjieff passed for a Seid, a descendant of the Prophet, singing religious verses in Persian while Skridlov, disguised as a dervish, beat the tambourine (pp. 230, c. 236).

    Afterlives

    Gurdjieff’s influence has run well beyond his pupils. Katherine Mansfield spent her last months at the Prieuré and died there in January 1923. Peter Brook filmed Meetings with Remarkable Men in 1979, and Keith Jarrett recorded a selection of the Gurdjieff–de Hartmann hymns as Sacred Hymns (1980).

    Henry Miller, who wrote the foreword to Fritz Peters’s memoir Boyhood with Gurdjieff, said in a filmed interview (“Henry Miller on G.I. Gurdjieff”) that of all the masters he had read about Gurdjieff seemed to him the most interesting, and the greatest enigma, precisely because he refused to be called a master: rather than be venerated, Miller says, he chose for twenty-one years to lead a “blameworthy” life instead of a praiseworthy one, an attitude close to the Sufi “path of blame” mentioned above (the gloss is mine, not Miller’s). To show what Gurdjieff could do, Miller retells a story from Peters’s book. Some three years after the car accident of 1924, Gurdjieff invited to a banquet at the Prieuré the gendarme who had found him unconscious by the road. The gendarme did not drink spirits; to prove that his armagnac could do no harm, Gurdjieff poured a full glass for the thirteen-year-old Peters, who drank it at a gulp, went on serving at table without a slip, and only five hours later, in bed, discovered that he was dead drunk. It is the Gurdjieff of Meetings all over: a lesson dressed up as hospitality, with the point left for the witness to work out.

    In Italy the most visible heir is Franco Battiato. In two interviews that circulate online (“Battiato incontra G.I Gurdjieff” and “Gurdjieff mi ha salvato la vita”, “Gurdjieff saved my life”) he tells how an existential crisis at the end of the 1960s, when he was about twenty-five, drove him “out of desperation” to invent his own metaphysical paths and to experiment, self-taught, with meditation; how he read the Indian mystics, Yogananda and Aurobindo; and how, around 1974–75, Ouspensky’s In Search of the Miraculous “set him alight”, because it explained systematically what he had found empirically on his own. He describes Gurdjieff’s system as an enormous one built for Western man in a consumer society, gathering the knowledge of many peoples and bringing it West, and he insists that a book is never enough: the spirit needs study as long and disciplined as ten years at the piano. Asked where a newcomer should begin, he recommends not Ouspensky but Meetings with Remarkable Men, which introduces Gurdjieff’s world “in the form of a novel” and is far easier to accept. The influence is audible in his songs. “Centro di gravità permanente” (“Permanent centre of gravity”, 1981) takes its title from Gurdjieff’s term for the stable inner aim that marks the beginning of real development; “Shock in My Town” (1999) draws on the additional shock needed to carry a process past the intervals of Gurdjieff’s law of the octave; and “Voglio vederti danzare” (“I want to see you dance”, 1982) summons the turning dervishes who haunt the book. Battiato also wrote an opera, Gilgamesh (1992), on the very epic that Gurdjieff’s father recited from memory; whether the choice owes anything to Gurdjieff, I cannot say.

    Le Règne de la quantité

    “A man is satisfied not by the quantity of food, but by the absence of greed.”

    One of his father’s sayings, Ch. II

    In 1945 René Guénon published The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times, which describes the modern world as the end of a long descent from quality to quantity: from things known by their nature to things counted, measured and sold, and from initiation to its counterfeits. Guénon had already written against Theosophy (1921) and spiritualism (1923), the very fashions Meetings dismisses. The opposition runs through Gurdjieff’s book as well, from its first pages. In the Introduction he tells how he gave up trying to reach his aim in writing by quantity and resolved to reach it by quality alone; and the elderly Persian whose speech fills most of the Introduction has a great deal to say about it.

    His speech is, at its centre, a tirade against journalism, which he calls the fundamental evil of modern life (p. 17). His cases are told with relish: a family of his acquaintance poisoned by sausages that paid reporters had praised for days, while the shop was unloading a spoiled consignment; an actress raised to fame by the reporter who had seduced her; a painter whose genius existed only in the newspapers; a writer and his wife driven to hang themselves by a critic whose advances she had refused (pp. 18–23). In ancient Babylon, he claims, the daily papyri were written only by elderly men of proven merit, appointed under oath, whereas now any whipper-snapper with a pretty style can be a journalist. It is a tirade, unfair to every honest reporter and nostalgic for a Babylon of its own invention, and yet on its central point it is hard to dispute. Paid praise dressed as information, notoriety manufactured on demand, critics with power and no responsibility, and above all the sheer quantity of print, which leaves the reader informed of everything and able to judge nothing: a century later, with advertising disguised as news and attention sold by the second, the Persian reads less like a crank than like an early witness.

    The Persian also has a word for the people at the top. The leaders of contemporary civilisation, he says, stand on a very low level of moral and psychic development and, like children playing with fire, cannot gauge what such literature does to the masses (p. 23). He does not call them charlatans: Meetings keeps that part, in effect, for journalists, critics and stage fakirs, and Gurdjieff admits without embarrassment that as a young man on the railway survey he took money from the “power-possessors” of small towns to bring the line their way (p. c. 86). A century on, I would put it more strongly. The arts the book mocks in the fakir, the promise of marvels, the staged certainty, the trade in hopes and fears, have passed into public life, and those who govern, or wish to, are among their most practised exponents.

    The other sign of the times is the direction of the search. Realisation is sought outside oneself: in a course, a retreat, an app that times one’s breathing, a teacher followed on a screen. Practices travel faster than ever, and they travel as Gurdjieff’s Hatha Yoga did, as procedures without understanding, which is precisely what the Persian dervish laughed at. Gurdjieff’s own vocabulary has gone the same way. The enneagram, which he presented to his Moscow pupils as a symbol of the laws governing any process, was turned from the 1970s, by Óscar Ichazo and Claudio Naranjo, into a typology of nine personalities, and now turns up in management training; self-observation returns as a wellness technique; and the “permanent centre of gravity” became the title of a hit song.

    The teaching itself has had two lives. One is quiet: the Gurdjieff Foundations of New York, Paris and London, built after his death under Jeanne de Salzmann, where the Movements are still taught and the ideas are passed on in small groups, in ordinary life rather than in monasteries, as Gurdjieff intended. The other is loud: books, seminars and groups trading on the name of the Fourth Way, some of them notorious for abusing their members. Gurdjieff is not wholly innocent of the second. He asked wealthy followers for money, by all accounts without embarrassment, played the master while refusing the title, and left behind a method easy to imitate and hard to practise. Guénon, for his part, placed him outside tradition altogether, as syncretic, without a lineage and without the rites of any religion; when Jeanne de Salzmann went to Cairo around 1950 to seek his approval, I have found no sign that she obtained it. By Guénon’s measure, Gurdjieff is himself one of the signs of the times.

    And yet the book answers the objection in its own way. Everything the market needs is missing from it: a method one could buy, a doctrine one could summarise, a master one could follow. Its teachers are met by chance and leave without instructions; its essential chapters were never written; its truths come wrapped in tall tales that each reader must see through alone. Gurdjieff was a merchant of genius and knew exactly what could be sold. Meetings looks to me like his attempt at the one thing that could not be: a book whose quality lies in what it refuses to quantify, and which hands back to anyone who comes to it for realisation the question of where to look.
